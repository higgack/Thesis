## 2. Theoretical background

### 2.1 Technology convergence

The idea that a technology developed in one domain spreads beyond its boundaries into other domains has a long lineage. Analyzing the emergence of the nineteenth-century machine tool industry, Rosenberg (1963) used the term technological convergence for the process by which firms came to recognize that machining techniques developed for firearms or sewing machines could also be applied to other products. Kodama (1992) later conceptualized technology fusion as the process of combining previously separate technological fields to create capabilities that none of them possessed alone. Hacklin et al. (2010) proposed a stage model in which convergence proceeds from the convergence of knowledge through the convergence of technologies and applications to the convergence of industries, and they argued that these stages form coevolutionary cycles through mutual feedback (Hacklin et al., 2009). Curran and Leker (2011) further proposed a method for observing these stages with patent indicators. Through these discussions, technology convergence research has become an established stream of research within technology and innovation management (Geum et al., 2016; Sick and Bröring, 2022), and empirical studies on the drivers of convergence have also begun to accumulate (Whang, 2018). Among them, Caviggioli (2016) analyzed the characteristics of the technological fields in which convergence is likely to occur, and Hwang (2020) used data on Korean information and communication technology firms to analyze the relationship between types of collaboration and convergence.

This prior research shares three features, which form the starting point of this study. First, its focus lies on overlaps between different industries or technological fields. Canonical cases such as telecommunications and computing, food and pharmaceuticals, and nanotechnology and biotechnology all involve firms from different industries discovering that their knowledge bases overlap (Gambardella and Torrisi, 1998; Bröring et al., 2006; No and Park, 2010). Second, convergence is generally measured with field-level indicators that aggregate co-classifications or citations between two fields (Curran et al., 2010; Cho and Kim, 2014; Song et al., 2017; Choi et al., 2022). Such indicators show whether and when two fields converge, but they cannot explain which inventions embody convergence or what characteristics such inventions have, because differences among individual patents are hidden in the average. Third, compared with the theory that views convergence as a staged process moving from knowledge through technologies and applications to industries (Hacklin et al., 2010), empirical studies have largely been confined to confirming, as a trend, whether the degree of convergence has increased. In other words, which technologies combine as convergence proceeds, and how the content and center of convergence change, have received relatively little attention. Organized around these three features, the following subsections review cross-stage technology convergence within the value chain (Section 2.2), the dynamic unfolding of technology convergence (Section 2.3), and the patent-based measurement of technology convergence (Section 2.4).

### 2.2 Cross-stage technology convergence within the value chain

With regard to the first feature of prior research, the boundaries at which convergence occurs do not exist only between industries. Within a single industry, there are other boundaries that separate the stages of the value chain, and the semiconductor industry is an appropriate case for examining them because these boundaries have long been an object of research on vertical specialization. Langlois and Steinmueller (1999) traced how competitive advantage shifted among firms and countries as the structure of the industry changed, and Macher and Mowery (2004) analyzed how fabless firms, foundries, and specialized assembly and test firms emerged as design was separated from fabrication and fabrication from assembly. Brown and Linden (2009) described how this structure has been reshaped through successive crises. According to Kapoor (2013), even as specialization advanced, some firms retained capabilities spanning multiple stages; these firms responded differently to technological transitions, and their choices shaped the architecture of the industry. In a study of the DRAM industry, Kapoor and Adner (2012) further showed that when components and systems are interdependent, firms benefit from possessing knowledge beyond the scope of what they produce themselves.

Research on vertical specialization has thus explained the formation and reorganization of the division of labor between process stages, and it connects with technology convergence research through the following logic. Industry architecture is the template that defines who does what and how value is distributed (Jacobides et al., 2006), and it enables specialization when the interfaces between process stages are stable. When a new technology makes the performance of one stage depend on the knowledge of another, however, the interface becomes unstable, and firms must acquire or integrate knowledge from the other side of the boundary. Such a combination of knowledge first appears at the level of invention: a single invention begins to incorporate the technologies of both stages, and patent co-classification can be regarded as a trace of this expansion of knowledge boundaries. Hybrid bonding is precisely a technology that makes the performance of one stage depend on the knowledge of another. Because its yield depends on fabrication capabilities that belong to the front end, such as planarization, surface chemistry, and alignment, the interface between fabrication and packaging is no longer stable. As a result, convergence occurs not between two industries but between two stages of a single industry. This phenomenon was anticipated in research on the limits of scaling (Arden et al., 2010; Iyer, 2016; Khan et al., 2018) and has been documented technically in packaging engineering research (Lau, 2021, 2022), but few methods have been proposed for observing it in the record of invention.

### 2.3 The dynamic unfolding of technology convergence

As the third feature of prior research identified in Section 2.1 suggests, the cross-stage convergence discussed above needs to be understood not as a fixed state but as a process that unfolds over time, because convergence is not a one-time event but a process that proceeds through several stages (Hacklin et al., 2010). Furthermore, according to the technology cycle model (Anderson and Tushman, 1990), a new technology passes through an era of ferment in which multiple designs compete before converging on a single dominant design. Combining the two perspectives, cross-stage convergence can also be expected to take different forms depending on the developmental stage of the technology. While the technology is forming, process inventions that make direct bonding possible will predominate; as the technology diffuses, cases in which process knowledge is combined with bonding inventions will increase. Once a single design approach has become established as the dominant design, the combination of the two technologies will shift toward the peripheral processes required for applications.

This perspective can also be applied to the technological development of hybrid bonding. According to stage models of convergence (Hacklin et al., 2010; Jeong et al., 2015), the form of convergence changes as a technology develops. Early hybrid bonding inventions concerned the process steps that made direct bonding possible, such as surface preparation, low-temperature annealing, and planarization, and were therefore often classified as process inventions. As the range of applications later expanded from wafer-level bonding for image sensors and 3D NAND to die-level bonding for high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and logic, the focus of invention shifted to bonded structures and stacking (Lau, 2021). Accordingly, bonding inventions can be expected to account for a growing share relative to fabrication-process inventions among more recent filings.

Moreover, changes in convergence may appear not only in the composition of application types but also in the relationships among the elements that make up a technology. A complex technology consists of core elements that are closely linked to other elements and peripheral elements that are not, and when the design of a core element changes, the other elements linked to it must change as well (Murmann and Frenken, 2006). The core of hybrid bonding is a connection scheme in which bonding pads are bonded directly to one another without bumps or solder (Lau, 2021). The extent to which inventions dealing with this core element also incorporate knowledge from the other side of the boundary is likely to vary with the developmental stage of the technology.

This difference can be explained from the perspective of the technology cycle model. In the era of ferment immediately after a new technology emerges, multiple designs compete, and the interfaces between the core element and the other elements are not yet settled. Once a dominant design has been established, however, the interfaces become entrenched, and subsequent invention shifts to incremental innovation that improves the established design (Anderson and Tushman, 1990). While the interfaces remain unsettled, inventing the direct connection of bonding pads requires dealing with fabrication-process knowledge such as surface planarization and wafer bonding. Once the interfaces have become entrenched, by contrast, such knowledge is taken as a given condition, and the core technology can be invented within the bonding domain. Brusoni et al. (2001) attributed the need for firms to possess knowledge beyond the scope of what they produce to unpredictable interdependencies among components. By the same logic, as interdependencies become predictable, the need for a single invention to incorporate knowledge from the other side of the boundary decreases. Because hybrid bonding was first commercialized in wafer-level bonding for image sensors and 3D NAND and its applications were later extended to die-level bonding (Lau, 2021, 2022), later filings are more likely to belong to a stage in which the design of the core technology has become entrenched. Applications containing the core technology can therefore be expected to incorporate fabrication-process knowledge frequently while the technology is forming, with this difference diminishing over time. The core technology is identified through the technology cluster and technology network analyses in Section 4.4.

**Building on this discussion, this study analyzes how the level and form of cross-stage convergence change over time and how the center of convergence shifts between core and peripheral technologies.**

### 2.4 Patent-based measurement of technology convergence

Empirically analyzing the level and form of the cross-stage convergence discussed above, and their change, requires an appropriate measurement method. Patents are the most widely used data for measuring convergence because classification symbols place each invention in one or more technological fields (Curran and Leker, 2011). Prior studies have generally measured convergence with field-level indices that count the patents to which symbols of two fields are jointly assigned (Cho and Kim, 2014; Jeong et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2015; Yun and Geum, 2019; Kwon et al., 2020). Other studies have used patent text to compensate for the limitation that classification systems reflect inventions only after the fact (Preschitschek et al., 2013; Zhu and Motohashi, 2022).

Of these two streams, this study belongs to the former in that it uses co-classification as a signal of convergence, but it differs in its unit of analysis. Instead of aggregating co-classification at the field level, this study classifies individual patents into three co-classification types: bonding-only, to which only bonding and interconnect symbols (H01L24) are assigned; process-only, to which only fabrication-process symbols (H01L21) are assigned; and integrated, to which both are assigned. Of these, the integrated type corresponds to patents that cross the boundary between process stages. Because this classification is defined at the level of the individual patent, it can be analyzed with standard discrete choice models, and it preserves the differences between patents that are averaged away in aggregate indices. This is intended to address the second feature of prior research noted in Section 2.1, namely, the limitation that field-level indicators can hardly explain differences among individual inventions. Note, however, that because CPC symbols are assigned by examiners according to the technical content of an invention, co-classification is a proxy for the fact that technologies from the two domains are included together in an invention, not a measure of the legal scope of the claims. This point is revisited in Section 4.2.

The identification of the core technologies of hybrid bonding and the construction of the variables that explain the co-classification types draw on the following two strands of patent research. The first is research on technology networks. If classification symbols are treated as nodes and pairs of symbols assigned to the same patent as links, the structure of the combinations among technological elements can be represented as a network (Sternitzke et al., 2008). The frequency with which two symbols are assigned together reflects the knowledge relatedness between the two technologies (Breschi et al., 2003), and a set of symbols that many patents share and that are strongly linked to one another, that is, a technology cluster, reveals the core elements of the technology. Using this approach, this study identifies the core technologies of hybrid bonding (Section 4.4) and analyzes whether patents containing them cross the boundary between process stages. The second is research on patent scope. Lerner (1994) measured patent scope by the number of classes a patent covers and showed that scope has economic value, and in the semiconductor industry, firms are known to build broader patent portfolios when markets for technology are more fragmented (Hall and Ziedonis, 2001; Ziedonis, 2004). The technological scope used in this study follows this tradition but is a measure limited to the number of detailed classifications within the two classes.

This technological scope is also closely related to the concept of technology convergence, because Kodama's (1992) technology fusion and Rosenberg's (1963) technological convergence both presuppose inventions that span two or more technological domains. From this perspective, applications that include more diverse detailed technical elements within the two classes should be more likely to be assigned symbols of both domains. This relationship, however, also has a mechanical aspect, because technological scope and the co-classification type are derived from the same set of CPC symbols and an integrated application by definition carries at least one symbol from each class, so its scope is at least two. This definitional difference in the minimum number of symbols is examined in an exploratory manner in Section 5.4 using the type-minimum-adjusted CPC scope. **Accordingly, along with the level of cross-stage convergence, this study analyzes the relationship between technological diversity and the integrated type.**
