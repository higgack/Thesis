## 1. Introduction

The division of labor in the semiconductor industry has persisted for more than half a century. The front end has formed transistors on the wafer, and the back end has diced finished wafers and assembled and packaged the resulting dies. According to Kapoor (2013), front-end fabrication became concentrated in large fabs operated by integrated device manufacturers and foundries, whereas packaging and testing were contracted out to outsourced semiconductor assembly and test providers (Macher and Mowery, 2004; Brown and Linden, 2009). This division of labor was possible because the interface between the two stages was managed in a relatively stable manner. [Stable interfaces allow production stages to specialize while maintaining distinct knowledge domains (Henderson and Clark, 1990; Brusoni et al., 2001).]{.mark} With the advent of advanced packaging, however, this interface has also begun to change: the performance of one stage has come to depend more directly on the process capabilities of the other. [When technological change destabilizes such an interface, innovation increasingly requires integrating specialized knowledge from both sides of the boundary (Grant, 1996).]{.mark}

Behind this change lie the limits of scaling. Scaling, which shrinks circuit linewidths, has run into a slowdown in density gains and a steep rise in costs. The semiconductor industry has therefore begun to seek new opportunities for performance gains in how dies are partitioned, stacked, and connected (Arden et al., 2010; Khan et al., 2018), and hybrid bonding is the technology at the center of this shift. According to Lau (2021, 2022), this technology, which directly bonds copper pads and the surrounding oxide without solder bumps, enables interconnect pitches below 10 µm that are difficult to achieve with conventional methods. Notably, the nanometer-scale surface planarization, surface activation, and precise alignment that determine its success are not traditional back-end capabilities but front-end fabrication capabilities. Moves by leading foundries to internalize advanced packaging capabilities can also be understood in the context of this deepening interdependence between process stages (Lau, 2022; KnowMade, 2024).

Hybrid bonding is thus a representative case of the merging of front-end and back-end technologies across their boundary, and this phenomenon can be understood from the perspective of technology convergence. Research on technology convergence has long examined how previously distinct technological domains come to overlap and combine (Rosenberg, 1963; Kodama, 1992; Curran and Leker, 2011; Sick and Bröring, 2022). However, prior research has focused mainly on overlaps between different industries or technological fields, such as information and communication technology and consumer electronics (Hacklin et al., 2009), food and pharmaceuticals (Bröring et al., 2006), and nanotechnology and biotechnology (No and Park, 2010). By contrast, convergence that cuts across the stages of the value chain within a single industry has received little attention. The closest precedent is the study by Karvonen and Kässi (2012), which analyzed patent citations between upstream and downstream firms in a value chain, but it, too, did not test at the patent level whether a technology crosses the boundary between process stages. Moreover, although convergence has been theorized as a staged process (Hacklin et al., 2010), empirical studies of how the content of convergence changes over time are scarce. Inventions that cross the boundary between process stages can be a technological starting point for changing the industry architecture that defines who produces what and how value is distributed (Jacobides et al., 2006); this research gap therefore needs to be addressed. [From a knowledge perspective, the question is not only whether the knowledge domains of the two stages converge but also how the locus of their integration changes as a technological architecture evolves.]{.mark}

This study accordingly analyzes whether convergence across the technological boundary between process stages can be observed at the level of individual patents and how this convergence unfolds over time. To this end, it uses 928 patent applications related to hybrid bonding extracted from the Worldwide Patent Statistical Database (PATSTAT) of the European Patent Office (EPO), together with the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) symbols assigned to each application. An application to which both a symbol of the **bonding and interconnect classification (H01L24)** and a symbol of the **fabrication-process classification (H01L21)** are assigned is defined as integrated, that is, as a patent that crosses the boundary between process stages; an application with only bonding and interconnect symbols is classified as bonding-only, and an application with only fabrication-process symbols as process-only. Capturing convergence through co-classification is itself a standard method used by Curran and Leker (2011), Kwon et al. (2020), and others. [Because an invention is a new combination of existing knowledge (Fleming, 2001), the co-classification of an application provides an observable, albeit imperfect, trace of the integration of knowledge from two process domains within an invention.]{.mark} What distinguishes this study is that it applies this method to the boundary between process stages within a single industry rather than between two industries and that it recasts co-classification as types of individual patents instead of aggregating it into field-level indices.

Specifically, this paper addresses the following three research questions (RQ1–RQ3). First (RQ1), to what extent and in what forms does convergence between fabrication-process and bonding technologies occur in hybrid bonding technology? Second (RQ2), how has cross-stage technology convergence changed over time? Third (RQ3), how has the center of cross-stage technology convergence been reorganized between core and peripheral technologies over time?

By answering these three research questions, this paper makes the following contributions. First, it extends the analytical scope of technology convergence research from convergence between industries or technological fields to technology convergence between different stages of the manufacturing process within the same industry. In doing so, it shows empirically that technology convergence occurs not only at industry boundaries but also at the technological boundaries of production processes. [In knowledge terms, it extends the study of knowledge boundaries to vertically separated process domains within a single industry.]{.mark} Second, it analyzes technology convergence not as a static combination but as a dynamic process that changes over time. The patterns of formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration observed in hybrid bonding patents show that technology convergence does not necessarily expand continuously and that the composition of convergence by type can change as a technology develops. [That is, the intensity and form of cross-stage knowledge integration are reorganized nonlinearly as the technology evolves.]{.mark} Third, it attends not only to quantitative changes in technology convergence but also to shifts in its structural center. By analyzing how the relationship between core bonding technologies and fabrication-process technologies varies over time, it provides empirical grounds for understanding the dynamic unfolding of technology convergence in terms of the core–periphery relationship of technological architecture and the technology cycle. [The results also suggest that, as the core design becomes entrenched, knowledge integration does not disappear but relocates from the core interface to the peripheral processes that implement it.]{.mark}
