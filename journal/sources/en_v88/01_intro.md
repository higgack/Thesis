## 1. Introduction

For more than half a century, the semiconductor industry has maintained a division of labor between the front end, which forms transistors on the wafer, and the back end, which dices the wafers and assembles and packages the dies. Front-end fabrication became concentrated in the fabs of integrated device manufacturers and foundries, whereas packaging and testing were contracted out to specialized assembly and test firms (outsourced semiconductor assembly and test, OSAT) (Macher and Mowery, 2004; Brown and Linden, 2009; Kapoor, 2013). This division of labor was possible because the interface between the two stages was stable. Stable interfaces allow production stages to specialize while maintaining distinct knowledge domains (Henderson and Clark, 1990; Brusoni et al., 2001), but when technological change destabilizes such an interface, innovation requires integrating specialized knowledge from both sides of the boundary (Grant, 1996).

Advanced packaging has brought precisely this kind of change. As scaling ran into slowing density gains and steeply rising costs, the industry began to seek performance gains in how dies are partitioned, stacked, and connected (Arden et al., 2010; Khan et al., 2018). Hybrid bonding, the technology at the center of this shift, directly bonds copper pads and the surrounding oxide without solder bumps and achieves interconnect pitches below 10 µm (Lau, 2021, 2022), and the nanometer-scale planarization, surface activation, and precise alignment that determine its success are front-end rather than back-end capabilities. Moves by leading foundries to internalize advanced packaging are also related to this deepening interdependence (Lau, 2022; KnowMade, 2024).

Research on technology convergence has examined how previously distinct technological domains come to overlap and combine (Rosenberg, 1963; Kodama, 1992; Curran and Leker, 2011; Sick and Bröring, 2022), but, as the cases of information and communication technology and consumer electronics (Hacklin et al., 2009), food and pharmaceuticals (Bröring et al., 2006), and nanotechnology and biotechnology (No and Park, 2010) show, its main interest has been in overlaps between different industries or technological fields. Even Karvonen and Kässi (2012), who analyzed patent citations between upstream and downstream firms in a value chain, did not test at the patent level whether an invention crosses the boundary between process stages. Moreover, although convergence has been theorized as a staged process (Hacklin et al., 2010), empirical studies of how the content of convergence changes over time are scarce. Because inventions that cross the boundary between process stages can be a starting point for changing the industry architecture (Jacobides et al., 2006), it matters not only whether the knowledge of the two stages combines but also how the locus of this integration changes as a technological architecture evolves.

This study analyzes cross-stage technology convergence, that is, the integration of knowledge from the two stages within an invention, at the level of individual patents. Using the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) symbols of 928 applications related to hybrid bonding extracted from the Worldwide Patent Statistical Database (PATSTAT) of the European Patent Office (EPO), it defines an application to which symbols of both the **bonding and interconnect classification (H01L24)** and the **fabrication-process classification (H01L21)** are assigned as integrated, and an application with symbols of only one of the two classes as bonding-only or process-only. Capturing convergence through co-classification is a standard method (Curran and Leker, 2011; Kwon et al., 2020), but this study applies it to the boundary between process stages within a single industry and recasts co-classification as types of individual patents instead of field-level indices.

The study addresses three research questions. First (RQ1), to what extent and in what forms is knowledge from the fabrication-process and bonding domains integrated within hybrid bonding inventions? Second (RQ2), how has cross-stage knowledge integration changed over time? Third (RQ3), how has the locus of cross-stage knowledge integration shifted between core and peripheral technologies over time?

The study makes three contributions. First, it broadens the scope of technology convergence research to convergence between process stages within the same industry and extends the study of knowledge boundaries, which has centered on firm boundaries, to vertically separated process domains. Second, it analyzes convergence as a dynamic process that passes through phases of formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration and shows that cross-stage knowledge integration is reorganized nonlinearly. Third, it provides empirical grounds for the argument that, as the core design becomes entrenched, knowledge integration does not disappear but relocates from the core bonding interface to peripheral processes. These findings show which process knowledge must be integrated, and where, as an innovation matures, which is relevant to managers and policymakers in other industries where downstream stages come to depend on upstream process capabilities.
