## 2. Theoretical background

### 2.1 Technology convergence and knowledge integration

Rosenberg (1963) used the term "technological convergence" for the process by which machining techniques developed for firearms or sewing machines came to be applied to other products through common production processes, and the machine tool industry, which accumulated this process knowledge, emerged as an independent industry. Kodama (1992) conceptualized technology fusion as the combination of previously separate technological fields to create capabilities that none of them possessed alone. From a knowledge-based perspective, such a combination is the integration of previously separate bodies of specialized knowledge (Grant, 1996), and each invention is a new combination of existing knowledge components (Fleming, 2001).

From case studies of 26 information and communication technology firms, Hacklin et al. (2010) derived a stage model in which convergence proceeds from knowledge convergence through technological convergence and applicational convergence to industrial convergence, and these stages form coevolutionary cycles (Hacklin et al., 2009). Not every instance of convergence passes through all four stages in sequence, but technological convergence, in which technologies from different fields are combined within a single invention, is well suited to observation with patents (Curran et al., 2010; Curran and Leker, 2011), and technology convergence research has since become established as a distinct stream in technology and innovation management (Caviggioli, 2016; Geum et al., 2016; Whang, 2018; Hwang, 2020; Sick and Bröring, 2022).

This prior research, however, shares three features. First, it focuses on overlaps between different industries or technological fields (Gambardella and Torrisi, 1998; Bröring et al., 2006; No and Park, 2010). Second, it measures convergence with field-level indicators that aggregate co-classifications or citations between two fields (Curran et al., 2010; Cho and Kim, 2014; Song et al., 2017; Choi et al., 2022); such indicators show whether and when two fields converge but cannot readily explain which inventions embody convergence. Third, whereas theory views convergence as a staged process, empirical studies have largely been confined to confirming whether the degree of convergence has increased, and how the content and center of convergence change has received little attention.

### 2.2 Cross-stage technology convergence and knowledge boundaries within the value chain

The boundaries at which convergence occurs do not exist only between industries. In the semiconductor industry, the separation of design, fabrication, and assembly gave rise to fabless firms, foundries, and specialized assembly and test firms (Langlois and Steinmueller, 1999; Macher and Mowery, 2004; Brown and Linden, 2009), yet firms that retained capabilities spanning multiple stages shaped the architecture of the industry (Kapoor, 2013), and in the DRAM industry, nonintegrated firms whose knowledge extended to the outsourced components gained performance advantages in technological transitions (Kapoor and Adner, 2012). The boundaries of production and of knowledge do not coincide, and in periods of technological change the gap between them determines how well firms can respond (Brusoni et al., 2001).

Industry architecture, which defines who does what and how value is distributed, can be reshaped as technology and firm strategy change (Jacobides et al., 2006). The division of labor between process stages persists as long as the interfaces between them are stable, but changes in how components are linked erode the value of established architectural knowledge (Henderson and Clark, 1990), and when a new technology makes the performance of one stage depend on the knowledge of another, firms must integrate knowledge from the other side of the boundary. Such integration can appear first in inventions that incorporate the technologies of both stages, before organizations and industry structure change.

Hybrid bonding is precisely a technology that makes the performance of one stage depend on the knowledge of another. Because its yield depends on front-end capabilities such as planarization, surface chemistry, and alignment, the interface between fabrication and packaging is no longer stable, as anticipated in research on the limits of scaling (Arden et al., 2010; Iyer, 2016; Khan et al., 2018) and documented in packaging engineering research (Lau, 2021, 2022), although few studies have observed this phenomenon in the record of invention. In terms of the stage model, knowledge convergence in hybrid bonding corresponds to the application of fabrication-process knowledge such as planarization and surface activation to chip-to-chip connection; technological convergence, to the combination of this knowledge with bonded structures within a single invention; applicational convergence, to the implementation of the combined technology in products such as image sensors, 3D NAND, HBM, and chiplets; and industrial convergence, to the reorganization of the division of labor, as when leading foundries internalize advanced packaging (Lau, 2022; KnowMade, 2024). Of these, only technological convergence can be observed directly through patent co-classification; applicational convergence is addressed only indirectly, through shifts in the locus of knowledge integration, and industrial convergence lies outside the scope of the analysis. If the interface has become unstable, inventions incorporating knowledge from both stages should not be exceptional, and such inventions should combine more diverse technical elements than inventions confined to one domain.

### 2.3 The dynamic unfolding of technology convergence and the locus of knowledge integration

In the technology cycle model (Anderson and Tushman, 1990), a technological discontinuity is followed by an era of ferment in which multiple designs compete, the emergence of a dominant design, and incremental change that refines the established design, and Hacklin et al. (2009) also explained convergence on the basis of an innovation cycle model. Combined with the stage model, this suggests that process inventions that make direct bonding possible should predominate while the technology is forming (knowledge convergence), that combinations of process knowledge with bonding inventions should increase as the technology diffuses (technological convergence), and that, once a dominant design has become established, the combination of the two technologies should shift toward the peripheral processes required for applications (applicational convergence). Indeed, many early hybrid bonding inventions concerned processes such as surface preparation, low-temperature annealing, and planarization, whereas the focus of invention later shifted to bonded structures and stacking (Lau, 2021). Patent-based studies of convergence dynamics have focused on how the degree of convergence between pairs of fields changes (Jeong et al., 2015; Yun and Geum, 2019) and have paid relatively little attention to how the composition of convergence by form changes within a single technology.

Changes in convergence may also appear in the relationships among the elements that make up a technology. Murmann and Frenken (2006) defined a dominant design as the stabilized design of the core elements, which, unlike peripheral elements, are closely linked to other elements and serve as stable interfaces, and once the core design has stabilized, subsequent technological change can continue in the peripheral elements, taking those interfaces as given. The core element of hybrid bonding is a connection scheme in which bonding pads are bonded directly to one another without bumps or solder (Lau, 2021), and inventing it in an era of ferment, when the interfaces are not yet settled, requires dealing with fabrication-process knowledge such as surface planarization and wafer bonding. Once a dominant design has been established, however, architectural knowledge is taken for granted (Henderson and Clark, 1990), and as interdependencies among components become predictable, the need to handle knowledge from the other side of the boundary decreases (Brusoni et al., 2001). Because hybrid bonding was first commercialized in wafer-level bonding and later extended to die-level bonding (Lau, 2021, 2022), later filings are more likely to belong to a stage in which the core design has become entrenched.

Inventions containing the core technologies can therefore be expected to incorporate fabrication-process knowledge more often than other bonding inventions while the technology is forming, but this difference should diminish as new combinations move to peripheral processes that take the interface of the core technologies as given. This is a relocation of the locus of knowledge integration from the core bonding interface to peripheral processes, that is, a transition from technological to applicational convergence. Furthermore, just as the convergence of process knowledge in Rosenberg's (1963) case spread through machine tools, processing apparatus can serve as an intermediary that connects the two stages; this study therefore divides fabrication processes into wafer fabrication, processing apparatus, and packaging-stage processes and compares the coupling of each domain with bonding technologies. These theoretical expectations are not tested as statistical hypotheses; they serve as benchmarks for interpreting the results.

### 2.4 Patent-based measurement of convergence as knowledge integration

Classification symbols place each invention in one or more technological fields and are therefore widely used to measure convergence (Curran and Leker, 2011). If classification symbols are treated as the technical components of an invention and their joint assignment as the recombination of components (Fleming, 2001), the joint assignment of symbols from different domains to a single patent is an observable trace of the combination of knowledge from the two domains within an invention, not a measure of knowledge transfer between organizations. Prior studies have aggregated co-classification into field-level indices (Cho and Kim, 2014; Jeong et al., 2015; Lee et al., 2015; Yun and Geum, 2019; Kwon et al., 2020) or have used patent text to compensate for the limitation that classification reflects inventions only after the fact (Preschitschek et al., 2013; Zhu and Motohashi, 2022).

This study instead classifies individual patents into three types. The process-only type represents fabrication-process knowledge for bonding that develops separately from bonding technology, and the integrated type represents knowledge from both domains combined within a single invention. A bonding-only application may be a bonding invention not yet combined with fabrication-process knowledge or one in which, the interface having become entrenched, such knowledge is taken as given, so type shares should be interpreted together with changes in composition over filing periods. Defining the types at the level of the individual patent allows discrete choice models to be applied and preserves differences between patents.

The core technologies are identified with technology cluster and network analyses (Section 4.4). Treating classification symbols as nodes and pairs of jointly assigned symbols as links represents the structure of combinations among technical elements (Sternitzke et al., 2008), and the frequency of joint assignment reflects knowledge relatedness (Breschi et al., 2003). Treating the most strongly linked symbols as core technologies approximates Murmann and Frenken's (2006) concept of core elements within this structure, and period-to-period changes in the symbols that link different symbols indicate the locus of knowledge integration. Technological scope adapts Lerner's (1994) count of classification symbols as a measure of patent scope, restricting it to subgroups within the two classes (Hall and Ziedonis, 2001; Ziedonis, 2004); because an integrated application by definition carries at least one symbol of each class, the relationship between technological scope and type has a mechanical aspect (Sections 4.3 and 5.4).
