## 1. Introduction

For more than half a century, the semiconductor industry has maintained a division of labor between the front end, which forms transistors on the wafer, and the back end, which dices finished wafers and assembles and packages the resulting dies. Front-end fabrication became concentrated in the large fabs of integrated device manufacturers and foundries, whereas packaging and testing were contracted out to outsourced semiconductor assembly and test providers (Macher and Mowery, 2004; Brown and Linden, 2009; Kapoor, 2013). This division of labor was possible because the interface between the two stages was stable. Stable interfaces allow production stages to specialize while maintaining distinct knowledge domains (Henderson and Clark, 1990; Brusoni et al., 2001), but when technological change destabilizes such an interface, innovation increasingly requires integrating specialized knowledge from both sides of the boundary (Grant, 1996).

Advanced packaging has brought precisely this kind of change. As scaling ran into a slowdown in density gains and a steep rise in costs, the semiconductor industry began to seek new opportunities for performance gains in how dies are partitioned, stacked, and connected (Arden et al., 2010; Khan et al., 2018). Hybrid bonding, the technology at the center of this shift, directly bonds copper pads and the surrounding oxide without solder bumps and achieves interconnect pitches below 10 µm (Lau, 2021, 2022). The nanometer-scale surface planarization, surface activation, and precise alignment that determine its success are not capabilities that the back end has traditionally accumulated but front-end fabrication capabilities, and moves by leading foundries to internalize advanced packaging capabilities are also related to this deepening interdependence (Lau, 2022; KnowMade, 2024).

Research on technology convergence has long examined how previously distinct technological domains come to overlap and combine (Rosenberg, 1963; Kodama, 1992; Curran and Leker, 2011; Sick and Bröring, 2022). As the cases of information and communication technology and consumer electronics (Hacklin et al., 2009), food and pharmaceuticals (Bröring et al., 2006), and nanotechnology and biotechnology (No and Park, 2010) show, however, its main interest has been in overlaps between different industries or technological fields. The closest precedent, the study by Karvonen and Kässi (2012), analyzed patent citations between upstream and downstream firms in a value chain, but it did not test at the patent level whether an invention crosses the boundary between process stages. Moreover, although convergence has been theorized as a staged process (Hacklin et al., 2010), empirical studies of how the content of convergence changes over time are scarce. This gap matters because inventions that cross the boundary between process stages can be a technological starting point for changing the industry architecture that defines who produces what and how value is distributed (Jacobides et al., 2006). From a knowledge perspective, the question is not only whether the knowledge domains of the two stages combine but also how the locus of their integration changes as a technological architecture evolves.

This study analyzes cross-stage technology convergence, that is, the integration of knowledge from the two stages within an invention, at the level of individual patents. The data are 928 patent applications related to hybrid bonding extracted from the Worldwide Patent Statistical Database (PATSTAT) of the European Patent Office (EPO), together with the Cooperative Patent Classification (CPC) symbols assigned to each application. An application to which both a symbol of the **bonding and interconnect classification (H01L24)** and a symbol of the **fabrication-process classification (H01L21)** are assigned is defined as integrated, that is, as a patent that crosses the boundary between process stages; an application with symbols of only one of the two classes is defined as bonding-only or process-only. Because an invention is a new combination of existing knowledge (Fleming, 2001), the co-classification of an application provides an observable, albeit imperfect, trace of the integration of knowledge from two process domains within an invention. Capturing convergence through co-classification is a standard method (Curran and Leker, 2011; Kwon et al., 2020), but this study applies it to the boundary between process stages within a single industry and recasts co-classification as types of individual patents instead of field-level indices.

The study addresses three research questions. First (RQ1), to what extent and in what forms is knowledge from the fabrication-process and bonding domains integrated within hybrid bonding inventions? Second (RQ2), how has cross-stage knowledge integration changed over time? Third (RQ3), how has the locus of cross-stage knowledge integration shifted between core and peripheral technologies over time?

The study makes three contributions. First, it extends the scope of technology convergence research to convergence between manufacturing process stages within the same industry and extends the study of knowledge boundaries, which has centered on firm boundaries, to vertically separated process domains. Second, it analyzes convergence as a dynamic process in which the composition of types changes through phases of formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration, and it shows that cross-stage knowledge integration is reorganized nonlinearly. Third, by tracing how the relationship between core bonding technologies and fabrication-process technologies varies over time, it provides empirical grounds for the argument that, as the core design becomes entrenched, knowledge integration does not disappear but relocates from the core bonding interface to the peripheral processes that implement it.
