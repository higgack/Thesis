## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Theoretical implications

**First, knowledge integration occurs not only between different industries but also between process stages within a single industry (RQ1).** The literature has treated convergence mainly as a phenomenon between industries or between technological fields (Hacklin et al., 2010; Curran and Leker, 2011; Zhang and Zhang, 2025). The finding that 45.8% of the applications related to hybrid bonding (37.2% under the narrow definition) carry symbols of both domains shows that the technological convergence stage of the stage model also appears in individual inventions at the boundary between process stages of a single industry. This extends the study of knowledge boundaries, which has centered on firm boundaries (Brusoni et al., 2001; Kapoor and Adner, 2012), to vertically separated process domains within an industry, and whereas Kapoor (2013) showed that organizational integration persisted amid the division of labor, this study shows the technological basis of that integration at the level of invention. Such inventions may precede the reorganization of the industry architecture (Jacobides et al., 2006), and whether the distribution of boundary-spanning patents can serve as a leading indicator of changes in organizational boundaries is left for future research. In addition, boundary-spanning patents included more diverse technical elements in all three phases, which offers one answer to the question of which inventions embody convergence. This finding is consistent with work on patent scope (Lerner, 1994) and on portfolio strategies in fragmented markets for technology (Ziedonis, 2004; Zhao et al., 2023), but it is a compositional association partly built into the definition of the types, not evidence that diversity causes integration (Section 5.4).

**Second, cross-stage knowledge integration did not expand linearly but unfolded through three phases of formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration (RQ2).** Table 7 summarizes the results in chronological order. If convergence were a simple process of expansion, the share of boundary-spanning patents should increase over time; instead, process-only applications were the most common in the formation phase and integrated applications in the diffusion phase, and in the reconfiguration phase bonding-only applications increased to a level similar to that of integrated applications. In terms of the expectations of Section 2.3, the formation phase corresponds to the knowledge convergence stage, the diffusion phase to the technological convergence stage, and the reconfiguration phase, in which the fabrication processes combined with the core technologies shifted to die support and encapsulation, to a transition toward the applicational convergence stage. This reveals the temporal change in the content of convergence that the stage model (Hacklin et al., 2010) proposed but that had received little empirical attention.

**Third, as the core design became entrenched, the locus of knowledge integration shifted from the core technologies to peripheral processes (RQ3).** The finding that the integrated-type advantage of applications containing the core technologies was pronounced in the formation phase but not observed in the reconfiguration phase, while the share of bonding applications containing them rose from 53.5% to 88.0%, is consistent with the technology cycle model, in which innovation after the establishment of a dominant design shifts to incremental improvement within that design (Anderson and Tushman, 1990), and with Murmann and Frenken's (2006) argument that once the design of a core element is entrenched, the need to change the elements linked to it diminishes. In light of Henderson and Clark (1990), the reduced visibility of knowledge integration at the core bonding interface may indicate not that this integration disappeared but that it became embedded in the established design. The core symbols remained hubs throughout the three periods, whereas the center of the fabrication processes combined with bonding, and the links between the two, shifted from wafer fabrication to processing apparatus and packaging-stage processes (Table 5), which echoes Rosenberg's (1963) observation that the convergence of production processes spread through machine tools. Here, "peripheral" means lying outside the core bonding symbols, not being less important in volume manufacturing. The maturation of a technological architecture thus relocates rather than eliminates knowledge integration across domains, which shows that the coevolutionary logic of Hacklin et al. (2009) also operates within a single technology.

### 6.2 Practical and policy implications

The finding that many applications contain both fabrication-process and bonding technologies implies that bonding capabilities alone are not sufficient in hybrid bonding and that knowledge of adjacent processes, spanning surface planarization, contamination control, surface chemistry, and alignment, may become increasingly important; because the boundaries of production and of knowledge do not necessarily coincide (Brusoni et al., 2001; Kapoor and Adner, 2012), firms need to maintain knowledge of adjacent process stages that they do not perform themselves. Moreover, in the reconfiguration phase, inventions containing the core technologies did not carry fabrication-process symbols more often than other bonding inventions, and the most frequent fabrication processes combined with them were processes for supporting dies and completing them as packages, which suggests that the focus of competition may be shifting from the core bonding principle to applications that combine this principle with die-level processes. For the Korean memory industry, which seeks to introduce hybrid bonding in next-generation HBM stacking, this implies that capabilities in handling and stacking thin dies may be required in addition to planarization, surface chemistry, and alignment metrology.

### 6.3 Limitations and future research

This study has several limitations. First, the H01L21–H01L24 co-classification is an observable trace of knowledge integration within inventions; it does not measure knowledge transfer between organizations, firms' absorptive capacity, or innovation performance. Because H01L21 includes packaging-stage processes and technological scope is derived from the same CPC information as the types, descriptive statistics such as the integrated share depend on the definition, although the regression results held under the narrow definition. Periodic CPC revisions and retroactive reclassification may also account for part of the observed change. Second, the title keyword search cannot capture applications whose titles lack relevant terms, and because the data lack patent family identifiers, duplicates were approximated with identical titles. Third, the regression data do not include applicants, firm types, nationalities, end-use areas, the distinction between wafer-level and die-level bonding, or CPC symbols outside the two classes. Fourth, this study does not claim causality. Diversity and type are likely to be jointly determined, and the period division approximates the emergence of a dominant design with filing periods whose boundaries were set in light of the concentration of filings. Because patent applications are generally published 18 months after their priority date, applications from the most recent years are not yet fully observed (Figure 2), and the results for the reconfiguration phase should be interpreted with the truncation of recent filings in mind. Fifth, CPC co-classification is reproducible but is assigned later than the invention itself, whereas text-based methods (Preschitschek et al., 2013; Lei et al., 2019; Zhu and Motohashi, 2022) allow earlier detection but depend on trained models.

Future research could apply the same two-class rule to other process technologies in which downstream stages have begun to depend on upstream capabilities, such as advanced lithography in displays, cell-to-pack integration in batteries, and additive manufacturing in aerospace assembly, to test whether the formation–diffusion–reconfiguration pattern is general. Examination under alternative period divisions and the use of generality indices (Petralia, 2020) to test the general-purpose character of hybrid bonding as it diffuses into logic, memory, and image sensors are also left for future work.

## 7. Conclusion

Technology convergence has been studied mainly as the combination of industries and as a question of whether the degree of convergence has increased. Understood as the integration of knowledge from previously separate domains, however, convergence can also occur between the stages of the value chain within a single industry. In the 928 applications related to hybrid bonding, integrated applications made up 45.8% (37.2% under the narrow definition) and included more diverse technical elements (RQ1), and knowledge integration passed through formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration phases as the center of gravity of filings shifted from fabrication processes toward bonding (RQ2). The odds that applications containing the core technologies also carried fabrication-process classifications were 11.1 times those of other bonding applications in the formation phase, but the difference was not significant in the reconfiguration phase, and the center of the fabrication processes combined with bonding shifted from wafer fabrication to processing apparatus and packaging-stage processes (RQ3). As the technological architecture matured, the integration of knowledge from the two process stages did not disappear but relocated from the core bonding interface to peripheral processes.

By extending the scope of technology convergence research to convergence between process stages within the same industry and recasting co-classification as types of individual patents linked to core technologies, this study connects research on technological innovation with research on knowledge integration across knowledge boundaries.
