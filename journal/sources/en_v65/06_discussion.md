## 6. Discussion

Based on the results in Section 5, this section discusses the theoretical, practical, and policy implications and presents the limitations of the study and directions for future research.

### 6.1 Theoretical implications

Corresponding to the three research questions, the results of this study have the following three theoretical implications.

**First, technology convergence occurs not only between different industries but also between process stages within a single industry (RQ1).** The existing literature has treated convergence mainly as a phenomenon between industries or between technological fields (Hacklin et al., 2010; Curran and Leker, 2011; Zhang and Zhang, 2025). The results of this study show that the same concept also applies to a boundary within an industry, namely, the boundary between the fabrication-process domain and the bonding domain of semiconductor manufacturing, and that the crossing of this boundary can be observed in individual patents. Of the patents related to hybrid bonding, 45.8% carry symbols of both domains, and even under the narrow definition, the share reaches 37.2%. If industry architecture is the template for the division of labor, as Jacobides et al. (2006) argue, then inventions that begin to incorporate both sides once the interface between stages becomes unstable reflect an expansion of technological interdependence that may precede the reorganization of that template; this study captures this expansion through patent co-classification. Whereas Kapoor (2013) showed that organizational integration persisted amid the division of labor, this study points to a possible technological basis for that integration, namely, that the knowledge of the two domains is being combined within individual inventions. According to the stage logic of convergence (Hacklin et al., 2010), the combination of technologies precedes the combination of organizations; the hypothesis that the distribution of boundary-spanning patents can therefore serve as a leading indicator of changes in organizational boundaries is left for future research.

This result also has implications for the characteristics of boundary-spanning inventions. In this study, boundary-spanning patents were found to include more diverse technical elements. Whereas Kodama (1992) defined fusion at the level of firms' R&D organizations and convergence indices operate at the level of fields, this study used patent-level evidence to identify technological diversity as a characteristic of boundary-spanning inventions. This provides one answer to the question raised in Section 2.1, namely, which inventions embody convergence, a question that field-level indicators could hardly address. Applications that span more CPC subgroups are more likely to carry both fabrication-process and bonding symbols, whereas applications with a narrow scope tend to remain within one domain. This association appeared in the formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration phases alike and thus persisted while the center of convergence shifted. This pattern accords with Lerner's (1994) finding that patent scope has economic value and with Ziedonis's (2004) explanation that broad portfolios are advantageous in fragmented markets for technology, although the value of patents is not measured in this study. In technologies whose performance and yield depend on the capabilities of adjacent process stages, broad technological diversity may also be related to intellectual property protection strategies (Zhao et al., 2023), and it is one way in which cross-stage convergence manifests itself in patent classification.

**Second, cross-stage convergence did not expand linearly but unfolded through three phases, namely, formation, diffusion, and reconfiguration, during which the center of gravity of filings shifted from fabrication processes toward bonding (RQ2).** Table 15 summarizes the results of this study in chronological order. If convergence were understood as a simple process of expansion, the share of boundary-spanning patents would be expected to increase steadily over time. The results, however, show otherwise. In the formation phase (1968–2014), stand-alone fabrication-process inventions, such as those on planarization, surface activation, and wafer bonding, were the most common type (process-only, 43.6%), and inventions dealing with the core of bonding mostly incorporated fabrication-process knowledge as well (OR of the core technologies, 11.071). In the diffusion phase (2015–2019), process inventions were more often classified together with bonding technologies than on their own, and the integrated share peaked at 62.2%. In the reconfiguration phase (2020–2024), the integrated share fell to 44.3%, and the bonding-only share rose to 45.4%. In this phase, applications containing the core technologies did not cross the boundary more often than applications without them (OR 0.658, not significant), and the fabrication processes most often assigned together with the core technologies were die support and encapsulation processes using temporary substrates. Meanwhile, the integrated share of bonding applications without the core technologies rose from 27.7% to 56.9%. The average number of symbols per application points in the same direction: the average number of bonding (H01L24) symbols increased from 3.03 in 1968–2014 to 4.73 in 2015–2019 and then decreased to 3.91 in 2020–2024, whereas that of fabrication-process (H01L21) symbols was 2.12 and 2.23 in the first two periods and then decreased to 1.46.

Whereas theory views convergence as a staged process moving from knowledge through technologies and applications to industries (Hacklin et al., 2010), temporal changes in the content of convergence have rarely been examined empirically; these results reveal such changes. That is, in hybrid bonding, technology convergence was not a static combination that, once formed, kept expanding but a dynamic process in which the level of convergence and its composition by type varied with the developmental stage of the technology. The background to these phase transitions can be understood more concretely through the following results on the shift in the structural center of convergence.

**Third, the center of convergence shifted from the core technologies to peripheral processes (RQ3).** The finding that the integrated-type advantage of applications containing the core technologies was pronounced in the formation phase but was not observed in the reconfiguration phase is consistent with the technology cycle model (Anderson and Tushman, 1990), according to which the interfaces become entrenched once a dominant design has been established and subsequent innovation shifts to incremental improvement within the established design. The increase in the share of bonding applications containing the core technologies from 53.5% to 88.0% can be interpreted as invention concentrating on a single core design, and the finding that inventions dealing with this core no longer incorporate fabrication-process knowledge more often than other bonding inventions is consistent with the logic of Murmann and Frenken (2006) that once the design of a core element has become entrenched, the need to change the elements linked to it decreases. As the use of hybrid bonding expanded from wafer-level to die-level bonding and as HBM and chiplets approached volume production (Lau, 2021, 2022), the point at which the two process stages meet appears to have moved from the bonding principle to processes for handling and stacking dies. The coupling strength of each fabrication-process domain and the betweenness centrality in the technology network show the same change. While the core symbols remained hubs throughout the three periods, the center of the fabrication processes combined with bonding, as well as the links connecting the two, shifted from wafer fabrication to processing apparatus and packaging-stage processes (Section 5.3, Table 11). Here, "peripheral" has the structural meaning of lying outside the core bonding symbols; it does not mean that these processes are less important in volume production. Cross-stage convergence thus did not accumulate through more patents crossing the boundary but unfolded through phase-by-phase changes in the content and center of what crosses the boundary. This pattern is consistent with the coevolutionary logic of Hacklin et al. (2009), here observed within a single technology, and it provides empirical grounds for explaining the dynamic unfolding of technology convergence in terms of the core–periphery relationship of technological architecture and the technology cycle.

### 6.2 Practical and policy implications

These results also have implications for firms and policy. The finding that a considerable number of applications include both fabrication-process and bonding technologies suggests that, in hybrid bonding, bonding capabilities alone are not sufficient and that knowledge of adjacent processes, encompassing surface planarization, contamination control, surface chemistry, and alignment, may become important. This is in line with the knowledge-boundary logic of Kapoor and Adner (2012) that firms benefit from possessing knowledge beyond the scope of what they produce themselves. The independent industry analysis by KnowMade (2024), which identifies foundries, integrated device manufacturers, and licensing firms as the applicants leading patent activity, can be understood in the same context.

Meanwhile, according to the period-specific results, inventions containing the core principle of hybrid bonding did not carry fabrication-process symbols more often than other bonding inventions in the reconfiguration phase, and the fabrication processes combined with the core technologies were often processes for handling dies and completing them as packages, such as support on temporary substrates and encapsulation. This suggests that the focus of competition may be shifting from the invention of the core principle to the invention of applied technologies that combine this principle with die-level processes. For the Korean memory industry, which seeks to introduce hybrid bonding into next-generation HBM stacking, these results imply that packaging competitiveness may not hinge on the bonding principle alone. In addition to fabrication-process capabilities such as planarization, surface chemistry, and alignment metrology, capabilities that extend to the handling and stacking of thin dies may be required.

### 6.3 Limitations and future research

The limitations of this study can be summarized in the following six points. The first concerns construct validity. H01L21–H01L24 co-classification is a proxy for cross-stage technology convergence, H01L21 also includes packaging-stage fabrication processes, and technological scope is derived from the same CPC information as the type. The regression results held under the narrow definition, but descriptive statistics such as the integrated share depend on the definition. In addition, because the CPC is revised periodically and past applications may be reclassified retroactively, part of the observed temporal change may reflect changes in the classification system and examination practices rather than in the technology itself. The second concerns sample selection. The title keyword search has clear criteria but cannot capture applications whose titles lack relevant terms, and the 68 irrelevant uses of the search terms had to be identified separately and examined through a sensitivity analysis. The third concerns the unit of observation and duplication. The unit of analysis is the application, and because the extracted data lack a patent family identifier, duplication was adjusted approximately on the basis of identical titles, so the independence of observations is not fully guaranteed. The fourth concerns omitted variables. The data do not include applicants and their firm types, nationality, end-use areas, or the distinction between wafer-level and die-level bonding, and CPC symbols outside the two classes are absent from the regression data. Because the indexing codes in the technology cluster and technology network tables come from a separate tabulation of all CPC symbols of the same applications, the core technology variable was defined using only the four bonding symbols included in the regression data. The boundary of the core technologies also depends on the threshold of 200 or more applications, although the substantive results held when the core technologies were restricted to the most strongly linked pair of core symbols (24/08 and 24/80). The fifth concerns causality. Because diversity and type are likely to be determined simultaneously and no suitable instrumental variable is available, no causal relationship is claimed, and filing office was included only as a control variable. The period division in the core technology analysis likewise approximates the timing of the formation of the dominant design by filing period rather than measuring it directly. The sixth concerns the timing of measurement. The CPC co-classification used as a signal of convergence is reproducible but is assigned later than the invention. Text-based methods (Preschitschek et al., 2013; Lei et al., 2019; Zhu and Motohashi, 2022) allow earlier detection but depend on trained models. Because the boundary between H01L21 and H01L24 is institutionally clear, this study prioritized reproducibility.

Despite these limitations, the design of this study can be applied to other contexts. Process technologies in which a downstream stage has begun to depend on upstream capabilities, such as advanced lithography in displays, cell-to-pack integration in batteries, and additive manufacturing in aerospace assembly, can all be analyzed with the same two-class rule and discrete choice models. A comparison of several cases would make it possible to determine whether the formation–diffusion–reconfiguration trajectory observed in this study is general. In addition, future research could exploit the fact that each patent is a set of several symbols and use representations that preserve higher-order co-occurrence or generality indices (Petralia, 2020) to test directly the general-purpose technology character of hybrid bonding as it spreads to logic, memory, and image sensors.

## 7. Conclusion

Technology convergence has been studied mainly as a combination between industries and as a question of whether the degree of convergence has increased. This study analyzed, at the level of individual applications, to what extent and in what forms technology convergence occurs between the process stages of a single industry, how its level and form change over time, and how its center shifts between core and peripheral technologies. To this end, each of the 928 applications related to hybrid bonding was classified as bonding-only, process-only, or integrated on the basis of its assigned CPC symbols, and this study estimated which patents cross the boundary between the two process stages and how this pattern changed over time.

The main results for each research question are as follows. First, regarding RQ1, cross-stage convergence was found not to be an exceptional phenomenon. The integrated type was the largest group (45.8%) and still accounted for 37.2% under the narrow definition, and patents including more diverse technical elements were more likely to cross the boundary. Second, regarding RQ2, the level and form of convergence changed through three phases. In the formation phase, stand-alone fabrication-process inventions (the process-only type) were the most common; in the diffusion phase, the integrated type, which includes both process stages, peaked at 62.2%; and in the reconfiguration phase, the integrated type declined, and the bonding-only type increased. As the process-only type became less common among more recent filings, the center of gravity of filings shifted toward bonding, and this result held in all re-estimations except for the *hybrid bonding* sample, which contains few applications filed up to 2014. The association between technological diversity and the integrated type persisted in all three phases. Third, regarding RQ3, the center of convergence shifted from the core technologies to peripheral processes. In the formation phase, the odds that applications containing the core technologies identified through the technology cluster and technology network also carried a fabrication-process classification were 11.1 times those of bonding applications without them, but this difference was not significant in the reconfiguration phase, and this decline held in all re-estimations. In addition, the fabrication processes assigned together with the core technologies changed to die support and encapsulation processes using temporary substrates, and the center of the fabrication processes combined with bonding also shifted from wafer fabrication to processing apparatus and packaging-stage processes.

These results suggest that cross-stage convergence was not a process of accumulation in one direction but a dynamic process in which the center of convergence moved from the core to the periphery as the design of the core technologies became entrenched.

The academic contributions of this study, corresponding to the three research questions, can be summarized as follows. This study extended the analytical scope of technology convergence research from convergence between industries to convergence between process stages within the same industry, analyzed technology convergence as a dynamic process whose composition by type changes over time, and empirically documented the shift in the structural center of convergence from the perspective of the core–periphery relationship of technological architecture and the technology cycle. Methodologically, the contribution of this study lies not in proposing a new convergence index but in recasting co-classification as types of individual patents, which makes it possible to estimate patent-level heterogeneity and its change over time, and in linking these types to the core technologies identified through the technology cluster and technology network.
